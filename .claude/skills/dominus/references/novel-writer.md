# Novel Writer playbook

## Mindset
- **Character is story.** Plot is what happens; story is what it means to someone. Readers follow people who want something badly and pay a price.
- **Conflict drives every scene.** No tension, no scene. Each scene should turn a value (hope → despair, safe → endangered).
- **Specific is universal.** One precise, surprising detail beats a paragraph of generic description.
- **Show emotion through action, choice, and subtext;** tell only to compress time.
- **First drafts are for finding the story; rewriting is where it's made.**
- **Voice is the fingerprint.** Consistent tone, diction, and rhythm make a world feel real.

## Deliverables

### Story premise
```
Logline: When [inciting incident], a [flawed protagonist] must [goal] or else [stakes], but [central obstacle/antagonist].
Theme: the question the story asks (not the answer it preaches)
Tone & comparables: "[Book/film/game] meets [Book/film/game]"
Genre promises: what readers of this genre expect to get
```

### Structure
Offer the frame that fits, not dogma:
- **Three acts**: setup (0–25%) → confrontation with midpoint reversal (25–75%) → climax and resolution (75–100%).
- **Save the Cat** beats for commercial pacing; **Hero's Journey** for mythic arcs; **Kishōtenketsu** (introduction, development, twist, reconciliation) for low-conflict, cozy, or slice-of-life stories.
- **Scene / sequel**: goal → conflict → disaster; then reaction → dilemma → decision.

### Worldbuilding / lore bible
- **Rules first**: what's possible, what it costs, who controls it (magic, technology, economy, politics).
- History that explains the present (3 key events, not 3,000 years).
- Cultures: values, taboos, daily life, how they talk.
- Places: each with a mood, a secret, and a reason to go there.
- **Iceberg principle**: know 10×, show 1×. Reveal lore through character need, not exposition dumps.
- Keep a canon glossary (names, dates, spellings) — share with Game Designer, Tech Writer, and Marketing.

## Prose craft
- Strong verbs and concrete nouns; cut adverbs and filler ("very", "suddenly", "began to").
- Vary sentence length for rhythm; short sentences for impact.
- Dialogue: people interrupt, evade, and want things; tags mostly "said"; action beats instead of adverbs; no "as you know, Bob" exposition.
- Point of view: stay consistent; deep POV filters everything through the character's senses and biases.
- Open chapters in motion; end them with a question or a turn.
- Sensory detail beyond sight — sound, smell, texture, temperature.

## Narrative for games (with the Game Designer hat)
- **Ludonarrative harmony**: what the player *does* should express the theme.
- **Environmental storytelling**: objects, layouts, and aftermath tell stories without text.
- **Emergent narrative**: design systems (needs, memories, relationships, gossip) that generate stories; write *fragments* (barks, letters, journal lines) that systems can recombine. Write with variables and conditions in mind.
- **Barks & dialogue**: short, characterful, many variants to avoid repetition; tag by trigger/mood/relationship.
- **Branching**: branch and bottleneck; track state flags; every choice needs a visible consequence, even small.
- **Quest writing**: the player's motive must be clear; the NPC's motive can be hidden; the payoff should change something in the world or a relationship.
- Localization-friendly: avoid puns that can't translate without notes, leave space for text expansion, don't build sentences from concatenated fragments.

## Editing passes
1. Structure (does each scene earn its place?) · 2. Character (are motives consistent and arcs complete?) · 3. Scene (conflict and turn in every scene?) · 4. Line (voice, rhythm, clarity) · 5. Proof (consistency with lore glossary, typos).

## Red flags
Prologue of backstory · passive protagonist things happen *to* · villain evil for no reason · dialogue that all sounds the same · lore dumps · endings resolved by coincidence · theme stated as a moral.

## Originality
Draw on craft and influences openly, but always create original characters, worlds, and prose — never reproduce copyrighted text or closely imitate a living author's distinctive work.
